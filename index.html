Project Abstract

Learn The Money is a human-centered financial literacy curriculum designed through design thinking methodology for teacher candidates and underserved populations. By applying Creative Confidence principles (Kelley & Kelley, 2013), this project transforms financial education from rote knowledge transmission to confidence-building, context-aware learning experiences that address real learner needs: money trauma, decision paralysis, and systemic barriers.

The artifact crosses disciplinary boundaries — integrating instructional design, behavioral economics, trauma-informed pedagogy, and design-based research — to produce a 10-week curriculum documented through reflective journal entries, a conjecture map, an extended explanation, a first-person design story, and a working interactive prototype. Each entry follows a structured 155 format (15 minutes to write, 5 minutes to read), making the iterative design process transparent and teachable.

Mission Statement: To equip educators with human-centered tools that foster financial self-efficacy, reduce money shame, and build confident, context-aware financial decision-making in underserved communities — one small experiment at a time.

Extended Explanation

What Is Learn The Money & Why Does It Matter?

The Problem

There is a persistent knowing–doing gap in financial literacy: adults can often explain budgeting or compound interest but still feel stuck, ashamed, or helpless about their own money decisions. Traditional programs emphasize content coverage while underestimating money trauma, shame, and learned helplessness — especially among underserved populations such as incarcerated women or students from chronically under-resourced communities.

On the educator side, many preservice and in-service teachers care deeply about financial capability but lack curricular resources, pedagogical models, and personal confidence to teach money topics. Together, these gaps represent both a problem and an opportunity to design learning experiences that center confidence and context, not just information.

Target Audience

The primary audience is teacher candidates and practicing educators who want to integrate financial literacy into their classrooms but feel under-prepared or anxious about teaching money topics. A secondary audience includes higher-education faculty and instructional designers in teacher preparation, adult education, or community-based programs — for whom the artifact serves as a live case study in applying design-thinking practices to a complex socio-emotional domain.

A third audience includes community partners — correctional education programs, nonprofits, and funders — who need a clear explanation of what makes this curriculum distinct from traditional budgeting workshops. All three audiences share a need for emotionally honest, context-aware, and research-grounded learning design.

How the Artifact Addresses the Need

First, the design journal models a design-thinking stance applied to financial literacy. Each entry uses a consistent 155 format — reading focus, key concepts, application, independent connections, reflection, next steps — making visible how abstract ideas (guided mastery, growth mindset, rapid experimentation) are translated into specific changes in activities, facilitation, and assessment.

Second, the journal keeps learners at the center by continually foregrounding specific populations. Entries connect course concepts and research to concrete Learn The Money scenarios, showing how human-centered design uncovers barriers that purely cognitive curricula miss — such as fear of public disclosure around debt or a belief that being "bad with money" is a fixed identity rather than a changeable skill.

Third, the artifact creates a transparent narrative of growth over time. By archiving early assumptions, evolving questions, and course corrections, it pushes back against perfectionism and highlights that meaningful curriculum emerges from many small leaps rather than a single breakthrough. Constraints are treated as design prompts rather than excuses to maintain the status quo.

Design Story — Parrish (2006)

First Day in Learn The Money

Perspective: Teacher candidate experiencing the curriculum for the first time. Present tense, first person.

I'm sitting in the back row, close enough to the door that I can leave if I need to. I tell myself I'm just here for the extra credit, but my stomach knows better. Any time someone says "financial literacy," my chest tightens. I've made too many mistakes with money to pretend this is neutral content.

The room feels different from what I expect. There's no big slide that says "Budgeting 101." Instead, the screen shows the words Money Stories and a simple prompt: When did money first feel powerful in your life? The facilitator — another grad student, not some distant expert — explains that today is about curiosity, not judgment. I'm not sure I believe them yet, but I notice that my shoulders drop a little.

We start with a quick anonymous poll on our phones: On a scale of 1–5, how confident do you feel talking about money with your students or future students? I tap 2 and feel a tiny wave of shame, imagining everyone else picking 4 or 5. The live results appear — most people are clustered around 2 and 3. I'm surprised, then relieved. I'm not the only one who feels under-prepared.

Next, we listen to a short story about a learner named Mara — a first-generation college student deciding between paying down credit card debt and helping family with rent. I feel myself leaning forward. I recognize the tension between being responsible and being loyal, between numbers on a spreadsheet and people you love. The facilitator doesn't tell us what Mara should do. They ask: "What feelings do you think she's carrying into a classroom conversation about money?" I jot down: fear of judgment, pride, fatigue, a quiet hope that someone might finally understand.

Then comes the part I dreaded: sharing. But the structure makes it feel safer. We work in pairs with a simple protocol. One person answers three prompts — my earliest money memory, one belief I was taught about money, a time money felt out of control — while the other just listens and reflects back emotions, not advice. We're told to stay in the present tense: I feel, I notice, I'm thinking, as if the memory is happening right now.

When it's my turn, I almost default to a joke, but my partner waits quietly. I hear myself say: "I'm eight years old, standing with my mom at the customer-service desk while she argues about an overdraft fee." I can feel the fluorescent lights, the crumpled receipt, my mom's voice tightening. I realize that a part of me still believes banks are like principals' offices — places you only go when you're in trouble.

My partner doesn't rush to fix it. She says, "I hear how small and responsible you feel at the same time." That sentence lands deeper than any financial tip I've heard in years. I notice that this activity isn't about budgeting yet — it's about making space for the emotions we drag into budgeting.

Later, we shift into designing. The facilitator introduces a Money Micro-Experiment card — a three-day spending-awareness challenge we can adapt for our own contexts. In small groups, we rewrite the instructions so they would make sense for different learners: ninth-graders, community-college students, or incarcerated women. We have to decide: what tone would feel respectful? What examples would feel real, not performative? We keep coming back to our own money stories as we argue about wording.

As we work, I notice a shift. I'm not just a student being taught about money — I'm a designer thinking about how money feels to other people. When someone suggests adding a "no shame" rule to the challenge, I feel a quiet click of recognition. If I'm this nervous in a graduate class, how must my future students feel?

We end with a brief reflection. On an index card, we answer two questions: What is one belief about money you want to examine more closely? and What is one tiny experiment you're willing to try this week? I write: Belief — "If I make a money mistake, it proves I'm irresponsible." Experiment — "Spend 20 minutes looking at my bank app without judging myself — just noticing." It feels almost laughably small, but also strangely radical.

Walking out, I notice that my chest feels lighter. Nothing about my bank account has changed in ninety minutes, but something about the story I tell myself has. For the first time, I can imagine bringing money into my future classroom not as a unit to survive, but as a conversation to design — carefully, collaboratively, and without shame.

Design Conjectures — Sandoval (2013/2014)

High-Level Design Conjecture Map

Following Sandoval's conjecture-mapping framework, Learn The Money treats the curriculum as an explicit set of hypotheses about how financial-literacy learning happens. The map specifies how design features are expected to work together to produce confidence, deeper understanding, and epistemic agency around money.

Learners develop deeper conceptual understanding, collaborative inquiry skills, and epistemic agency when the learning environment integrates cognitive scaffolding, real-world contextual connections, moral-value awareness, and aesthetic experience — enacted through structured collaborative inquiry with iterative design and argumentation tasks.
(Wilson's Four Pillars · Sandoval, 2014)
Conjecture A — Tools & Materials

Money Micro-Experiments

If learners engage in iterative Money Micro-Experiments using authentic tools within small groups that share epistemic responsibility, they will produce stronger evidence-based justifications and engage in more generative arguments about financial choices.

Conjecture B — Discursive Practices

No-Shame Norms

If discursive norms for consensus-building, no-shame storytelling, and demands for justification are consistently enacted, learners will internalize healthier epistemic standards for talking about money and apply them spontaneously beyond the course.

Conjecture C — Task Structure

Real-World Role Context

If tasks connect explicitly to real-world contexts and roles — future teachers, returning citizens, caregivers — learners will perceive financial literacy as relevant, increasing motivation and quality of knowledge application.

Conjecture D — Aesthetic Dimension

Emotionally Resonant Design

If aesthetic dimensions — emotionally resonant narratives, clear visual design, and thoughtfully paced experiences — are deliberately designed, learners will experience deeper, more memorable engagement that supports conceptual change and sustained confidence.

Logic Model Summary

Inputs: Facilitators, authentic financial scenarios, Money Micro-Experiment cards, reflective prompts, collaborative digital tools.

Activities: Collaborative inquiry labs, argumentation workshops, money-story discussions, design-council sessions, iterative prototype testing.

Outputs: Revised activity cards, reflection journals, peer-feedback artifacts, pre/post confidence surveys, conjecture map revisions.

Short-term outcomes: Increased financial self-efficacy, reduced money shame, greater willingness to discuss money in classroom settings.

Long-term impact: Lasting financial agency, ethical and context-aware instructional design practice, scalable community-facing financial literacy model.

ENTRY 1

FLIP: From Design Thinking to Creative Confidence

Week 1 — January 26, 2026

Reading Focus: Chapter 1 — "FLIP: From Design Thinking to Creative Confidence."

Key Concepts Learned: The Kelley brothers describe "FLIP" as the shift from seeing creativity as rare to seeing it as a learnable capacity. The chapter introduces the three-circle design thinking framework — Feasibility, Viability, Desirability — and uses the Doug Dietz MRI case to show how human-centered design can dramatically change experiences for vulnerable users.

Application to Learn The Money: Traditional financial literacy often stays in feasibility (math, content) and viability (products) while ignoring desirability — the fears, hopes, and lived realities of learners. For Learn The Money, I need to flip my own practice so I start with empathy for teacher candidates and incarcerated women, then design content around their real contexts rather than abstract standards.

Reflection: The growth-mindset lens helps me see "I'm bad with money" as a belief, not a fixed trait. If I expect creative confidence from my learners, I also have to model it by sharing in-progress work and inviting feedback instead of waiting until everything is polished.

Next Steps: I will conduct short empathy interviews with 3–5 teacher candidates to surface their money stories, anxieties, and prior experiences. These insights will guide my first round of prototype activities.

Citation: Kelley & Kelley (2013), Ch. 1 Read time: ~5 min
ENTRY 2

DARE: From Fear to Courage

Week 2 — February 2, 2026

Reading Focus: Chapter 2 — "DARE: From Fear to Courage" in Creative Confidence.

Key Concepts Learned: The authors reframe fear of failure as a normal part of creative work and highlight Bandura's guided-mastery research, showing how confidence grows through a series of small, successful steps. Creative people do not fail less — they run more experiments and interpret each attempt as learning rather than personal judgment.

Application to Learn The Money: Many learners in my programs carry money trauma and shame, which makes them fearful of public mistakes. I want to design low-stakes practice — tiny financial experiments like a one-week spending log or a $5 automated transfer — that allow learners to experience quick, concrete wins and start rewriting their internal stories about being "bad with money."

Independent Connections: This chapter connects directly to Bandura's self-efficacy work and Dweck's growth mindset research. In a financial-literacy context, that means building structures that help learners see evidence of their own competence, not just hear explanations from an expert.

Personal Reflection: I recognized my own perfectionism as a fear pattern — I delay curriculum prototypes because I want them to be publication-ready. DARE nudges me to treat each version of Learn The Money as a prototype and to make my "messy middle" visible rather than hiding it.

Next Steps: I will design one safe-to-fail roleplay where teacher candidates practice a difficult money conversation, then debrief what felt empowering or uncomfortable.

Citation: Kelley & Kelley (2013), Ch. 2; Bandura (1997); Dweck (2006) Read time: ~5 min
ENTRY 3

SPARK: From Blank Page to Insight

Week 3 — February 10, 2026

Reading Focus: Chapter 3 — "SPARK: From Blank Page to Insight."

Key Concepts Learned: The chapter emphasizes generating lots of ideas before evaluating them, reframing challenges with "How might we?" questions, and using field observations and empathy interviews to uncover needs that data alone can't reveal. The Embrace Infant Warmer case demonstrates how reframing from "low-cost incubator" to "baby-warming solution for rural mothers" opened up an entirely new product direction.

Application to Learn The Money: Instead of asking "How do I teach all the required money topics?", I'm reframing to "How might we create financial confidence through small, meaningful experiments?" This pushes me to observe real contexts — teacher-candidate classrooms, correctional-education settings — and design activities that fit those realities.

Independent Connections: SPARK echoes design-thinking practices from the d.school and aligns with Lusardi's research showing that context and behavior matter as much as content knowledge.

Personal Reflection: I saw how often I open a blank document and wait for the perfect curriculum idea instead of using prompts, constraints, or rough sketches to get started. Adopting a traveler's mindset — noticing friction points in everyday money experiences — gives me more raw material to design from.

Next Steps: This week I will conduct three empathy interviews with teacher candidates, focusing on moments when they felt stuck or successful with money. I'll turn their stories into at least five "How might we?" questions and use those as seeds for new Learn The Money activities.

Citation: Kelley & Kelley (2013), Ch. 3; Lusardi (2010); d.school resources Read time: ~5 min

LEAP: From Planning to Action

Independent Connections: LEAP connects to lean-startup ideas about minimum viable products. It also mirrors the gap in financial literacy where people already know they should budget or save but struggle to act — reinforcing Lusardi's findings that knowledge alone is insufficient.

Next Steps: This week I will prototype one Money Micro-Experiment activity that teacher candidates can run with peers or family — such as a three-day spending-awareness challenge — and collect quick feedback using two prompts.

TEAM: Creatively Confident Groups

Week 6 — March 3, 2026

Reading Focus: Chapter 6 — "TEAM: Creatively Confident Groups" in Creative Confidence.

Key Concepts Learned: The Kelley brothers argue that creative work is almost always a team sport. Strong teams deliberately build psychological safety so people feel safe sharing half-baked ideas, balance diverse perspectives instead of privileging a single expert, and create rituals that keep ideas moving. Leaders act more like gardeners than heroes — their job is to create conditions where others can flourish.

Application to Learn The Money: Learn The Money cannot depend on one charismatic facilitator — it needs teams of teacher candidates, community educators, and even learners who co-create experiences. That means building norms and structures that make money conversations feel safe in a group. For example, I can embed specific team rituals into the curriculum: a no-shame rule revisited every session, rounds where everyone shares one small experiment, and rotating roles so quieter participants still have defined ways to contribute.

Independent Connections: This chapter connects to Amy Edmondson's work on psychological safety and to research on professional learning communities in teacher education.

Personal Reflection: I often treat curriculum design as a solo project, then later ask for feedback when most major decisions are already locked in. TEAM pushes me to involve others much earlier — inviting teacher candidates, correctional educators, and formerly incarcerated women to react to rough ideas, not just polished lesson plans.

Next Steps: Over the next two weeks I will pilot a small design council for Learn The Money — a group of 3–5 people including at least one teacher candidate and one community educator — who meet briefly to react to new Money Micro-Experiments.

Citation: Kelley & Kelley (2013), Ch. 6; Edmondson (1999) Read time: ~5 min

Prototype & Iterate: Testing Creative Approaches

Week 9 — March 24, 2026

Reading Focus: Prototype testing and iteration across the Creative Confidence framework, connected to the design conjecture map and logic model.

Key Concepts Learned: Prototyping is not just about making an activity visible — it is about making assumptions visible. A prototype gives learners something concrete to respond to, which is often more useful than asking them abstract questions about what they might like. Iteration is the disciplined practice of revising the design in response to what learners actually do, say, and produce.

Application to Learn The Money: In Learn The Money, this means I cannot judge a financial-literacy activity only by whether the instructions are clear or the content is accurate. I need to test whether the activity actually lowers shame, invites reflection, and helps learners make more confident decisions about money. The prototype is successful only if it works in the emotional and social reality of the learners, not just in my planning document.

Independent Connections: This week helped me see a stronger link between classroom prototyping and design-based research. In the conjecture map, the prototype is part of the embodiment of design, while learner conversations, revised worksheets, and reflection responses become evidence of mediating processes.

Personal Reflection: I noticed that I still have a tendency to become attached to activities I spent a long time designing. When feedback suggests that something is confusing or emotionally heavy, my first instinct is to explain the activity better rather than rethink it. This entry reminded me that iteration requires humility — if learners misread or resist a prototype, that is valuable information about the design, not a failure of the learner.

Next Steps: I will test at least one Learn The Money prototype with a small group and document three kinds of evidence: what learners say, what they produce, and what they avoid. I will then revise the activity using those findings and note which part of the conjecture map the revision is addressing.

Tentative Research Plan

Learn The Money: Research Roadmap

This plan positions Learn The Money as a foundation for future design-based research, pilot testing, and possible dissertation or grant development. The artifact is the most developed prototype to date — not a finished product.

Overarching Purpose & Guiding Questions

The overarching purpose of the study is to examine how a human-centered financial literacy curriculum can support financial self-efficacy, willingness to engage in money conversations, and epistemic agency. Three guiding questions frame the work:

  1. How does participation in Learn The Money influence learners' financial self-efficacy and confidence in discussing money-related topics?
  2. Which features of the curriculum design appear to support or constrain learner reflection, emotional safety, and real-world transfer?
  3. How do iterative revisions, informed by learner data and conjecture mapping, improve the effectiveness of the intervention across cycles?
Theoretical Framework

Design-Based Research & Conjecture Mapping

Grounded in DBR (Sandoval, 2014) and Wilson's Four Pillars. The study treats Learn The Money as both an instructional intervention and a theory-building design experiment.

Methodology

Mixed Methods, Iterative DBR Cycles

Quantitative measures provide evidence of change in confidence or self-efficacy. Qualitative data explains how participants experience the design. Findings drive revisions across cycles.

Participants

Teacher Candidates & Adult Learners

Initial participant pool: teacher candidates, graduate students, or community-based adult learners. Later phases may extend to community workshops or additional underserved populations.

Data Sources

Surveys, Artifacts & Observation

Pre/post surveys, short reflective journal responses, observational notes, learner artifacts, facilitator memos, and feedback on prototype activities.

Qualtrics Integration

TAMU-CC Infrastructure

Qualtrics supports pre-assessment, embedded feedback after prototype activities, and post-assessment using branching logic, display logic, and downloadable data exports.

Potential Measures

Self-Efficacy Instrument

Short financial self-efficacy instrument paired with custom reflection items related to money shame, classroom confidence, and action planning — triangulated with learner work samples and facilitator observations.

Anticipated Contribution

Community-Facing Model

This project models an accessible, low-cost research infrastructure for graduate student researchers studying community-facing educational interventions that integrate pre/post assessment with DBR iterative cycles.

Potential for Extension

Future Development Roadmap

Learn The Money is designed as an evolving, research-informed model. The following phases outline realistic plans for iterative development, testing, refinement, scaling, and integration into larger research or grant projects.

Phase 1 — Summer 2026

Small Pilot Study

Conduct a small pilot cycle with 8–12 teacher candidates or adult community learners. Deploy the Qualtrics-based pre/post confidence survey alongside 2–3 prototype Money Micro-Experiment activities. Collect observational notes and learner artifacts. Use data to make targeted revisions to the conjecture map.

Phase 2 — Fall 2026

Design Council Co-Design

Invite a small design council of 4–6 participants — including teacher candidates, community educators, and at least one formerly incarcerated woman — to co-interpret pilot findings and co-design the next iteration of curriculum activities. Integrate feedback into a revised prototype.

Phase 3 — Spring 2027

Expanded DBR Cycle

Run a full design-based research cycle with a larger participant group across two contexts (teacher preparation program and a community-education setting). Apply updated conjecture map. Conduct comparative analysis of pre/post data, learner artifacts, and observation notes across both sites.

Phase 4 — 2027–2028

Dissertation or Grant Proposal

Use accumulated data, revised conjecture map, and evidence from DBR cycles to develop a dissertation proposal or external grant application. Position Learn The Money as a scalable, community-facing financial literacy model grounded in creative confidence, trauma-informed pedagogy, and design-based research methodology.
