Short Abstract

Why This Project Exists

Learn The Money is a human-centered financial literacy curriculum built through design thinking methodology for teacher candidates and underserved populations. By applying the Creative Confidence framework from Kelley & Kelley (2013), the project transforms financial education from rote knowledge transmission into confidence-building, context-aware learning experiences that address real learner needs — including money trauma, decision paralysis, and systemic barriers.

The curriculum centers authentic money narratives, low-stakes experimentation through Money Micro-Experiments, and collaborative facilitation that reduces shame and invites reflection. Rather than delivering content from a position of expert authority, it positions educators as co-designers who surface learners' existing knowledge and lived experience as the starting point for financial growth.

The goal: equip educators with research-informed tools to foster financial self-efficacy, epistemic agency, and sustained confidence in their students and communities — documented through this ten-week design journey drawing on design-based research, conjecture mapping, and Wilson's Four Pillars of instructional design.

Primary Audience

Teacher Candidates & Adult Learners

Preservice and in-service educators, incarcerated women, community-based adult learners, and underserved college students carrying money shame, trauma, or systemic financial barriers.

The Artifact

What Learn The Money Is

A web-based instructional artifact and curriculum framework designed to be facilitated across formal, community, and correctional education settings.

Intended Learner Experience

Learners do not enter a budgeting lecture. They enter a designed conversation. The first session begins not with a spreadsheet but with a simple prompt — "When did money first feel powerful in your life?" — delivered in a facilitated environment with explicit no-shame norms, anonymous polling, and narrative-sharing protocols that redistribute authority from instructor to community.

From there, learners progress through structured Money Micro-Experiments: three-day spending-awareness challenges, $5 automated-saving experiments, money-story card sorts, and peer-review of financial decisions. Each activity is designed so that small, successful steps accumulate into genuine self-efficacy — drawing directly on Bandura's guided-mastery model (1997).

The artifact supports both pedagogical and andragogical dimensions. For teacher candidates, it models how financial literacy instruction can be equity-centered, low-threat, and discussion-rich. For adult learners, it respects prior experience, foregrounds self-direction, and connects directly to each learner's immediate financial reality rather than abstract standards.

Core Instructional Features

  • Money Stories Protocol: Structured narrative sharing using first-person present tense, reducing shame and building community
  • Money Micro-Experiments: Low-stakes, time-bound financial experiments designed to build self-efficacy through action, not advice
  • Interactive Calculators: Embedded tools for monthly payment and savings-goal planning, grounding abstract concepts in learners' real numbers
  • No-Shame Norms: Explicitly designed facilitation norms that reframe financial "mistakes" as data, not character judgments
  • Conjecture-Aligned Reflection: Structured prompts tied to the design conjecture map, enabling iterative curriculum improvement
  • Facilitator Co-Designer Role: Educators are positioned as empathic co-designers, not content authorities, using empathy interviews and peer protocols

What Makes This Artifact Creative & Distinct

Most financial literacy programs operate on a deficit model: identify gaps in knowledge, fill them with content, test recall. Learn The Money crosses disciplinary boundaries by fusing instructional design, design thinking, narrative psychology, and community-based participatory research into a single facilitation model.

The artifact is novel in its insistence that financial confidence is not a knowledge state — it is a designed condition. This reframe treats the Feasibility–Viability–Desirability triangle from IDEO not as a product-development tool, but as a lens for curriculum: what is financially feasible for this learner, what is systemically viable, and — crucially — what is desirable given their lived emotional context?

Wilson's Four Pillars in Practice

1

Cognitive Scaffolding

Reading frameworks, "How might we?" reframes, and structured argumentation tasks build knowledge incrementally

2

Real-World Context

Activities embedded in learners' actual financial situations — not hypothetical scenarios — demand authentic thinking

3

Moral & Value Awareness

No-shame norms and value-laden financial dilemmas (e.g., debt vs. family duty) foreground ethics alongside economics

4

Aesthetic Experience

Emotionally resonant money narratives, thoughtful visual pacing, and carefully designed peer protocols create memorable learning

Design Process

How the Artifact Was Built

A systematic, iterative development process grounded in design-based research and the Creative Confidence framework — from initial empathy to functional prototype.

The Problem & Opportunity

Two overlapping gaps motivated this project. On the learner side, a persistent knowing–doing gap characterizes financial literacy: adults can often articulate concepts like compound interest or budgeting but still feel stuck, ashamed, or helpless about their own money. Traditional programs emphasize content coverage and assessment while underestimating money trauma, shame, and learned helplessness — especially among incarcerated women, first-generation college students, and chronically under-resourced communities.

On the educator side, many preservice and in-service teachers care deeply about financial capability but lack curricular resources, pedagogical models, and personal confidence. Together, these gaps represent an opportunity to design learning experiences that center confidence and context, not merely information.

Audience & Context

Empathy interviews with teacher candidates revealed that money anxiety is nearly universal but rarely discussed in preservice training. Learners described feeling underprepared to facilitate money conversations, fearing that their own financial struggles would undermine their credibility as future educators.

In correctional-education settings, the design constraints expand: learners carry acute financial trauma, have limited access to banking systems, and face re-entry challenges that make abstract financial planning feel cruel rather than helpful. These realities were treated as design fuel — constraints that forced the curriculum to be more specific, more emotionally responsive, and more actionable than a standard budgeting workshop.

Design Process Phases

1

Empathize & Define (Weeks 1–2)

Applied the FLIP and DARE lenses from Kelley & Kelley (2013) to reframe the design challenge from "cover the content" to "build financial confidence." Conducted informal empathy interviews with 3–5 teacher candidates to surface money stories, anxieties, and prior knowledge. Key insight: learners' internal narratives about money were more powerful barriers than any knowledge gap.

2

Ideate & Reframe (Weeks 3–4)

Used SPARK methods to generate "How might we…?" questions from learner interviews. Reframed from "How do I teach all the required money topics?" to "How might we create financial confidence through small, meaningful experiments?" Generated over 20 candidate activities, then selected Money Micro-Experiments as the core structural innovation based on fit with Bandura's guided-mastery model and Lusardi's (2010) context-behavior research.

3

Prototype & Test (Weeks 5–7)

Developed initial prototype handouts, facilitation scripts, and interactive web tools. Applied LEAP principles to run small, fast pilots: one 15-minute activity tested with peers, a spending-awareness card tested informally, and calculator tools iterated based on usability feedback. Each cycle produced written reflection using a consistent 15/5 entry format, creating a transparent record of design decisions and rationale.

4

Integrate & Refine (Weeks 8–9)

Applied conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014) to formally articulate how design elements were expected to produce target learning outcomes. Refined activity language and no-shame norms in response to peer feedback. Introduced the design story format to make the learner experience legible to outside stakeholders. Integrated TEAM-based facilitation protocols to support group epistemic responsibility.

5

Document & Scale (Week 10)

Consolidated all design artifacts — journal entries, conjecture map, design story, research plan, interactive prototype — into this public-facing portfolio. Drafted a tentative research plan for future design-based research cycles and identified Qualtrics-based infrastructure for systematic data collection in future pilots.

Feedback Integration

Feedback was gathered through three mechanisms across the design cycle:

  • Informal peer review: Prototype activities shared with graduate-student peers using two open prompts — "What helped you feel more confident?" and "What would you change?" — producing direct revisions to activity language and sequencing
  • Design journal reflection: The 15/5 entry format required explicit "Next Steps" at each entry, creating a documented trail of how feedback influenced subsequent design decisions
  • Instructor feedback integration: Course feedback on design conjectures prompted revision of the conjecture map's mediating-process layer and clarified the logic between design features and intended outcomes
Design Justification

Theory, Design, & Outcome Alignment

Extended explanation, design conjecture map, and design story — the theoretical and narrative scaffolding that explains why the artifact is built the way it is.

Extended Explanation

This web-based design portfolio documents the iterative development of Learn The Money, a human-centered financial literacy curriculum informed by design thinking and the Creative Confidence framework. Across ten weeks, the site traces how ideas move from theory to prototype to implementation in ways that are transparent, research-grounded, and teachable to others.

The primary audience — teacher candidates and practicing educators — encounters the artifact as a concrete example of how a fellow educator wrestles with ambiguity, fear, and institutional constraints while still moving a curriculum project forward. For higher-education faculty and instructional designers in teacher preparation and adult education, the site functions as a live case study in applying design-thinking practices to a complex socio-emotional domain: money.

The artifact responds to a persistent knowing–doing gap in financial literacy. Adults can often explain concepts like budgeting or compound interest but still feel stuck, ashamed, or helpless about their own financial decisions. Traditional programs emphasize content coverage and quizzes while underestimating money trauma, shame, and learned helplessness — especially among incarcerated women or students from chronically under-resourced communities.

By archiving early assumptions, evolving questions, and course corrections, the portfolio pushes back against perfectionism and communicates that meaningful curriculum emerges from many small leaps rather than a single breakthrough. Constraints — limited class time, institutional expectations, learners' life circumstances — are treated as design prompts rather than excuses to maintain the status quo.

High-Level Design Conjecture

Learners develop deeper conceptual understanding, collaborative inquiry skills, and epistemic agency when the learning environment is designed around authentic, reflective practice that integrates cognitive scaffolding (Wilson's Pillar 1), real-world contextual connections (Pillar 2), moral-value awareness (Pillar 3), and aesthetic experience (Pillar 4) — enacted through structured collaborative inquiry with iterative design and argumentation tasks.

Outcomes

Intended Results

Financial self-efficacy, transferable epistemic agency, critical awareness of social and value dimensions, sustained motivation.

Working Design Conjectures

Conjecture 1

If learners engage in iterative Money Micro-Experiments using authentic tools within small groups that share epistemic responsibility, then they will produce stronger evidence-based justifications and engage in more generative arguments about financial choices.

Conjecture 2

If discursive norms for consensus-building, "no-shame" storytelling, and demands for justification are consistently enacted, then learners will internalize healthier epistemic standards for talking about money and apply them spontaneously beyond the course.

Conjecture 3

If tasks are structured to connect explicitly to real-world contexts and roles — future teachers, returning citizens, caregivers — then learners will perceive financial literacy as relevant, increasing motivation and quality of knowledge application.

Conjecture 4

If aesthetic dimensions — emotionally resonant narratives, clear visual design, thoughtfully paced experiences — are deliberately designed, then learners will experience deeper, more memorable engagement that supports conceptual change and sustained confidence.

Design Story

First Day in Learn The Money

Written from the perspective of a teacher candidate experiencing the curriculum for the first time. First-person present tense, per Parrish (2006) design-story methodology. Intended outcomes: financial self-reflection, reduced money shame, recognition of money as a designable conversation.

I'm sitting in the back row, close enough to the door that I can leave if I need to.

I tell myself I'm just here for the extra credit, but my stomach knows better. Any time someone says "financial literacy," my chest tightens. I've made too many mistakes with money to pretend this is neutral content.

The room feels different from what I expect. There's no big slide that says "Budgeting 101." Instead, the screen shows the words Money Stories and a simple prompt: When did money first feel powerful in your life? The facilitator — another grad student, not some distant expert — explains that today is about curiosity, not judgment. I'm not sure I believe them yet, but I notice that my shoulders drop a little.

We start with a quick anonymous poll on our phones: "On a scale of 1–5, how confident do you feel talking about money with your students or future students?" I tap 2 and feel a tiny wave of shame, imagining everyone else picking 4 or 5. The live results appear: most people are clustered around 2 and 3. I'm surprised, then relieved. I'm not the only one who feels underprepared.

Next, we listen to a short story about María, a first-generation college student trying to decide between paying down credit card debt and helping family with rent. As the story unfolds, I feel myself leaning forward. I recognize the tension between being responsible and being loyal, between numbers on a spreadsheet and people you love. The facilitator doesn't tell us what María "should" do. Instead, they ask, "What feelings do you think she's carrying into a classroom conversation about money?" I jot down: fear of judgment, pride, fatigue, and a quiet hope that someone might finally understand.

Then comes the part I dreaded: sharing. But the structure makes it feel safer. We work in pairs with a simple protocol — one person answers three prompts while the other listens and reflects back emotions, not advice. When it's my turn, I almost default to a joke, but my partner waits quietly. I hear myself say: "I'm eight years old, standing with my mom at the customer-service desk while she argues about an overdraft fee." As I describe the scene, I realize that a part of me still believes banks are like principals' offices — places you only go when you're in trouble.

My partner says, "I hear how small and responsible you feel at the same time." That sentence lands deeper than any financial tip I've heard in years. I notice this activity isn't about budgeting yet — it's about making space for the emotions we drag into budgeting.

Later, we shift into designing. In small groups, we rewrite a Money Micro-Experiment so it makes sense for different learners — ninth-graders, community-college students, incarcerated women. We keep coming back to our own money stories as we argue about wording. When someone suggests adding a "no shame" rule, I feel a quiet click of recognition. If I'm this nervous in a graduate class, how must my future students feel?

We end with two questions on an index card: "What is one belief about money you want to examine more closely?" and "What is one tiny experiment you're willing to try this week?" I write: Belief: If I make a money mistake, it proves I'm irresponsible. Experiment: Spend 20 minutes looking at my bank app without judging myself — just noticing. It feels almost laughably small, but also strangely radical.

Walking out, I notice that my chest feels lighter. Nothing about my bank account has changed in ninety minutes, but something about the story I tell myself has. For the first time, I can imagine bringing money into my future classroom not as a unit to survive, but as a conversation to design — carefully, collaboratively, and without shame.

Evidence of Development

Ten-Week Design Journey

A curated synthesis of the design journal — ten entries documenting how theory was translated into prototype decisions, feedback was integrated, and the curriculum evolved across weekly cycles.

Design Milestones

W1
January 26, 2026

FLIP — From Design Thinking to Creative Confidence

Established core theoretical foundation: Kelley & Kelley's three-circle framework (Feasibility, Viability, Desirability). Key insight: traditional financial literacy ignores desirability — the emotional and contextual realities of learners. Committed to empathy-first design.

W2
February 2, 2026

DARE — From Fear to Courage

Applied Bandura's guided-mastery model to the curriculum design. Designed initial Money Micro-Experiment concept: low-stakes financial experiments (one-week spending log, $5 automated transfer) that allow learners to build self-efficacy through small, successful steps rather than expert instruction.

W3
February 10, 2026

SPARK — From Blank Page to Insight

Generated "How might we…?" reframes from initial empathy interviews. Reframed core question from "How do I teach the required content?" to "How might we create financial confidence through small, meaningful experiments?" Identified money-story narrative as the primary entry point.

W4
February 16, 2026

LEAP — From Planning to Action

Addressed the knowing–doing gap in the designer's own practice. Committed to running fast, small pilots rather than waiting for a "complete" curriculum. Tested one prototype activity with peers; collected feedback using structured prompts. First iteration of Money Micro-Experiment card produced.

W5
February 23, 2026

SEEK — From Duty to Passion

Connected personal design motivation to the project's equity mission. Identified the communities — teacher candidates, incarcerated women — most poorly served by existing financial literacy curricula. Refined the audience statement and deepened the no-shame facilitation norm.

W6
March 2, 2026

TEAM — Creatively Confident Groups

Designed collaborative facilitation protocols: distributed expertise roles, peer-review structures, and consensus-building dialogue norms. Applied group-level creative confidence principles to reduce individual performance pressure in money-related discussions.

W7
March 9, 2026

MOVE — Creative Confidence to Go

Translated creative confidence from classroom activity into sustained behavioral change. Prototyped the "one tiny experiment" reflection exit card as a bridge between in-class learning and real-world financial action.

W8
March 16, 2026

Integration — Design Thinking in Financial Literacy

Synthesized the seven Creative Confidence chapters into a coherent facilitation model. Articulated the formal conjecture map following Sandoval (2014). Made design hypotheses explicit and traceable to specific curriculum elements.

W9
March 23, 2026

Prototype & Iterate — Testing Creative Approaches

Conducted structured prototype review with peer feedback. Revised facilitation language, added the money-story sharing protocol, and refined the no-shame norm cards. Drafted the design story and confirmed its alignment with Parrish (2006) design-narrative methodology.

W10
March 30, 2026

Reflection & Moving Forward

Final reflection and research plan development. Documented the complete design trajectory, drafted research questions for future DBR cycles, and produced this public-facing artifact portfolio as the primary deliverable and proof of learning.

Used "How might we…?" methodology to transform the design challenge. Potential for Extension

Research Plan & Future Development

This artifact is positioned as the most developed prototype in an ongoing design-based research trajectory — one that can support systematic data collection, iterative redesign, and deeper scholarly inquiry.

Guiding Research Questions

1
How does participation in Learn The Money influence learners' financial self-efficacy and confidence in discussing money-related topics — with students, peers, and in their own lives?
2
Which features of the curriculum — Money Micro-Experiments, no-shame norms, money-story protocols, or facilitator positioning — appear to support or constrain learner reflection, emotional safety, and real-world transfer?
3
How do iterative revisions — informed by learner data and conjecture mapping — improve the effectiveness of the intervention across design-based research cycles?

Proposed Methodology

This study will be grounded in design-based research (DBR) and guided by conjecture mapping, treating the curriculum as a set of testable hypotheses about how financial-literacy learning happens. A mixed-methods, multi-cycle approach will be used.

Quantitative measures (pre/post financial self-efficacy surveys, confidence check-ins) will provide evidence of change, while qualitative data (reflective journal responses, observational notes, learner artifacts, facilitator memos) will explain how participants experience the design. TAMU-CC's Qualtrics platform provides practical infrastructure for pre-assessment, embedded activity feedback, and post-assessment — enabling a low-cost, replicable research workflow accessible to graduate-student researchers.

The initial participant pool will include teacher candidates and graduate students in education programs. Later phases will extend to community workshops and correctional education settings, with each phase producing revised conjectures and updated activities.

Anticipated Scholarly Contribution

This project contributes to instructional design and educational technology by demonstrating how financial literacy can be designed as a confidence-building, context-aware, and emotionally responsive learning experience — not merely a content-delivery problem. It models an accessible DBR infrastructure for graduate researchers studying community-facing educational interventions.

Future Development Pathways

Pilot Implementation

Formal pilot with teacher candidates in a TAMU-CC education course, collecting pre/post self-efficacy data and reflective journals across 3–4 sessions of selected Learn The Money activities.

Community & Correctional Education

Adapt the curriculum for community workshop settings and correctional education programs, with context-specific modifications to materials, norms, and facilitator training — addressing the re-entry financial landscape directly.

Dissertation & Grant Development

Position the conjecture map, research questions, and pilot findings as foundation for a dissertation proposal or competitive grant application exploring financial self-efficacy through DBR across underserved adult-learner populations.

Facilitator Training & Scaling

Develop a facilitator guide and training module so community partners, nonprofits, and educators can adopt Learn The Money without the original designer present — enabling sustainable, distributed implementation.
